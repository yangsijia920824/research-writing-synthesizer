# Sentence Synthesis

## Start With Meaning

Define the intended semantic components before selecting language:

```text
A = established context
B = specific limitation
C = consequence or need
```

Do not begin by paraphrasing the first sentence found.

## Comparison Method

When one example already contains the needed components:

1. Select the strongest candidate.
2. Compare it with another same-purpose example.
3. Keep the clearer structure or replace only the stronger component.
4. Continue through the candidate set.
5. Stop when additional examples no longer improve precision, logic, or fit.

This is a practical tournament-style comparison, not a vote by frequency.

## Induction Method

When no single example contains all components:

1. identify which source best expresses each component;
2. verify that the components are logically compatible;
3. choose a grammatical structure that can hold them;
4. rebuild the sentence around the user's study;
5. check that the combined claim is supported, not merely linguistically smooth.

## Work at Four Levels

1. **Sentence:** argumentative function and overall claim.
2. **Clause or phrase:** causal, contrastive, conditional, or descriptive units.
3. **Skeleton:** grammatical relation among units.
4. **Leaves:** domain terms, verbs, modifiers, and measurements.

Borrow insight from examples at different levels, but do not preserve a long source-specific sequence.

## Candidate Strategy

- First pass: retain one to three strong candidate structures.
- Second pass: compare against remaining examples and refine.
- Usually two to four genuinely different examples are sufficient.
- Avoid collecting exhaustive synonyms that do not change the decision.
- Use `/` for temporary alternatives and resolve them in the final draft.

## Structural Rewriting

Replace more than isolated vocabulary. Consider changing:

- clause order;
- grammatical subject;
- active versus passive construction;
- noun versus verb emphasis;
- sentence boundaries;
- explicit versus implicit causal relation;
- level of specificity;
- comparison frame.

Adapt all wording to the user's actual material, model, method, and result.

## Logic and Transitions

Choose transitions only after naming the relation:

- addition;
- contrast;
- concession;
- cause;
- consequence;
- refinement;
- example;
- temporal or procedural sequence.

Do not insert `however`, `nevertheless`, or similar markers merely because source papers use them.

## Originality Heuristics

During drafting, visually mark long retained spans so they receive another rewrite pass. A practical source method suggests paying attention to runs of roughly five or more identical words and to concentrated borrowing from one source. Treat these as editing heuristics, not universal plagiarism thresholds or legal rules.

Generic short phrases may not require expression-level provenance, but every factual claim, quantitative statement, and attributed interpretation requires a verified source.

## Final Sentence Test

- Does it express the intended A+B+C meaning?
- Is every factual element supported?
- Does the syntax accurately represent causality and uncertainty?
- Is it tailored to this study rather than the source paper?
- Does it duplicate the previous or next sentence?
- Is any retained wording longer or more distinctive than necessary?
- Is the citation attached to the claim it actually supports?
