# Labeling and Outline Construction

## Label by Writing Purpose

A label should reveal why a passage exists in the argument. Prefer:

```text
[existing treatment limitation]
```

over:

```text
[treatment]
```

Do not translate each sentence into a label. One label may cover a sentence, a related sentence set, or a paragraph.

## Choose Granularity

A useful label is:

- specific enough that the passage's function is clear without rereading it;
- broad enough to avoid a unique label for every sentence;
- more detailed for material near the user's core contribution;
- coarser for background that is unlikely to enter the final draft.

Keep distinct labels when passages differ in argumentative function even if they discuss the same topic.

## Preserve Boundaries

Keep:

- source identity;
- article boundaries;
- paragraph boundaries;
- sentence grouping;
- citation provenance.

Use an empty marker such as `[ ]` to indicate the boundary between labeled and not-yet-labeled material in an incremental workflow.

## Build the Three Views

### Complete Map

Duplicate each label as a heading and place its source passage beneath the second copy. Keep this view unchanged. A doubled label count can be used as a mechanical integrity check when following this representation.

### Working Queue

Copy the complete map. This is the only view from which evidence units are moved during outline construction.

### Outline Workspace

Create top-level headings for candidate writing points. Derive them by comparing label sequences across papers and asking which moves are necessary for the user's argument.

## Infer Rather Than Impose

Common moves can include:

- broad importance or burden;
- current system or practice;
- limitation or unmet need;
- desired capability;
- prior strategies;
- limitation of prior strategies;
- remaining gap;
- why the gap matters;
- practical or translational criterion;
- study strategy;
- this work.

This is a menu, not a template. The corpus may support repeated problem -> solution -> new problem cycles, a direct comparison structure, a mechanism-first structure, or another sequence.

## Populate the Outline

1. Scan labels through the navigation or structured index.
2. Create a heading for each necessary writing point.
3. Cut relevant labeled passages from the working queue into the matching heading.
4. Let the residual queue shrink as a visible unprocessed list.
5. When one passage belongs under multiple headings, copy it from the complete map rather than moving it again from the working queue.
6. Preserve plausible alternative outlines until the writing goal makes the choice clear.

## Count Invariant

For movable labeled units:

```text
units remaining in working queue
+ units moved into outline workspace
= units in complete map
```

Adapt the exact count to the representation, but preserve the invariant: nothing should disappear silently.

## Argument Tests

For each heading, ask:

- How does this move prepare the reader for the core contribution?
- Is it necessary, or merely interesting?
- Does the next move follow logically?
- Does the gap explain consequences or significance?
- Is the claimed gap based on a sufficient search?
- Are disagreements or conflicting findings visible?

The final outline should be contribution-centered, evidence-backed, and shorter than the complete map.
